# Actual-play study: silly tables, serious moments

What a long-running actual-play table does when players joke and when the story turns heavy, measured across every transcript on the Critical Role wiki and written down for the Seventh Bell and Waypoint. These notes are in our own words and quote nothing.

## What was read

- **All 595 transcripts** on the wiki's transcript list: Campaigns 1 to 3, one-shots, live shows and the shorter series. That's 572 usable episodes and 1.7 million spoken lines.
- **About 18 DMs.** Matthew Mercer ran 419 episodes, Brennan Lee Mulligan 54 and Aabria Iyengar 20; cast members and guests ran the other 79, mostly one-shots. (The DM is taken to be whoever speaks most.)
- **Laughter tests** used the 424 to 563 episodes where the transcribers marked laughter. Each episode was measured from its first game section, after the announcements.
- **Turns into minutes.** A typical episode has about 2,900 game lines in three and a half hours, so 14 lines is roughly a minute. Minutes below are estimates.
- **Close reading.** Nine episodes were also read closely at the turns where the tone changed: a first session, a death and the session after it, light episodes, a dragon negotiation, and two finales.

The earlier version of these notes rested on those nine alone. The full count confirmed some of what they suggested and overturned two claims; both are marked below.

## How a table handles silly

1. **Bits are short and mostly end by themselves.** Three in four are a single laugh. When a bit runs to two or more laughs, a quarter end with no word from the DM: the players drift back into the game themselves. Another 42% end on a short DM answer or reaction. The DM rarely needs to stop anything.
2. **When the DM moves things on, he narrates.** Of the bits the DM ends, the big lever is a long piece of narration that moves the fiction on: time passes, something appears, someone arrives (13% of bits). He uses it about as often as everything else put together: a short "all right" or "so" (9%), an NPC speaking (7%), a roll request (3%) or calling the next turn (2%). None of these scolds anyone.
3. **Players bring most of the laughter.** Four in five laughs follow a player's line. The DM gets one in five, usually by playing an NPC straight.
4. **Fights are only a little less funny.** Stretches that are mostly combat get 0.38 laughs per 50 lines, against 0.54 elsewhere. Jokes in a fight release tension; they don't break it.
5. **Shopping, travel and gear are where banter runs.** The close reading showed the DM giving clear options there (pace, miles, what the cart costs) and letting the table argue them out.

## How a table handles serious

1. **Seriousness lives in quiet scenes, not fights.** About five times an episode the table goes 20 minutes or more without laughing, and 95% of those stretches are mostly not combat: conversations, investigations, confessions, grief. The quiet scene is the one to protect.
2. **A character dropping changes the room, by different amounts.** After a character's first death saving throw (83 episodes), the next laugh took a median of 165 lines (about 12 minutes), 1.7 times the episode's usual gap. The middle half ranged from 0.6 to 4.3 times. Sometimes the table jokes at once out of nerves; sometimes it goes silent for half an hour. Neither needs managing.
3. **The DM states the consequence plainly and briefly.** The close reading showed no lingering description of the death. Players accepted the loss because the dice were open and the danger had been signalled.
4. **The players lead the grief.** In the session after the death, the players acted it out: anger at an ally, taking back the dead character's belongings, choosing at once to give chase. The DM's lines got very short, and he answered questions without steering.
5. **Lightness returns through the players, not a planted NPC.** *(Overturned.)* The nine-episode notes said warmth came back through a newly met NPC. Across 2,090 long serious stretches, players broke the silence 80% of the time, the same rate as any laugh, and an NPC's voice only 4%. Nobody has to plan the relief; the table finds it.
6. **Saying "nobody is to blame" out of character is rare.** *(Narrowed.)* It appears clearly in only a handful of the 424 episodes, most plainly straight after the death in Found & Lost. It's a tool for real losses, not a routine.

## Structure

- **Recap first.** Half the episodes (52%) open play with a "last we left off" recap within the first few lines. In the close reading, the recap named any loss plainly before handing the scene to the players.
- **Break in the middle, never mid-fight.** The break falls at 48% (80% between a third and two thirds of the way in). Only 3% come straight after a fight; usually the break follows a decision or a reveal.
- **Finales end with an epilogue for each character.** In the close reading, each player got their character's ending, jokes and tears alternated quickly, and the DM narrated one closing image for each.

## Spotlight

The quietest regular player speaks at about two thirds of an equal share. In 27% of episodes, at least one regular goes 300 lines (about 20 minutes) without speaking. With seven regulars that's expected, but at a smaller table a quiet player is easier to miss and matters more.

## What we built from this

**Campaign, revision 7.8:**
- Sessions 1 to 3 gain a **Bring it back** line for each scene: one in-fiction move (time, a sound, a change of light, someone arrives) for when a bit doesn't end by itself.
- Each of those sessions also gains a **Where to break** line, after the mid-session reveal and never mid-fight.
- Sessions 2 and 3 gain an **Opening recap** script that names any loss.
- Session zero gains **Silly and serious**: let bits land, protect the quiet scenes, and what to do when a Toll is paid or a character falls.
- The finale gains an **Epilogue round**.

**Waypoint 1.44.0:**
- **Bring it back:** the session notes bar shows the scene's line, and names the character who has had the least spotlight tonight.
- **A spotlight count:** one tap per character when they take the scene.
- **A break hint:** the session clock says when you're at the planned break.
- **A heavy-moment marker:** "Heavy moment" joins Mark this moment, and when you use it, a short care card appears.
- **Quick help** gains two situations: "The table drifts" and "A heavy moment".
- **Opening recap:** Foresight's prep brief drafts a "last we left off" from the previous session's chronicle and heavy moments.
